Passage 1　Bioluminescence in the Deep Sea
Bioluminescence — the production of light by living organisms — is the rule rather than the exception in the deep ocean. In waters below about 200 meters, where sunlight no longer reaches, scientists estimate that the majority of animals generate their own light. The light comes from a chemical reaction in which a molecule called luciferin is oxidized with the help of an enzyme, luciferase. Because the reaction releases almost no heat, it is extraordinarily efficient.
Deep-sea creatures use this light for several purposes. The anglerfish dangles a glowing lure above its mouth to attract prey, while certain squid eject clouds of luminous fluid to confuse predators and escape in the darkness. Many species also use light to communicate: the distinctive patterns of photophores along a fish's body help individuals recognize members of their own species and find mates in an environment where visibility is poor.
Perhaps the most common defensive use is counterillumination. Animals such as the hatchetfish carry light organs on their undersides and adjust their brightness to match the faint light filtering down from the surface. Viewed from below, a predator sees not a dark silhouette but a patch of water that looks much like any other, and the intended meal disappears.

Passage 2　Coffeehouses in Seventeenth-Century Europe
The first coffeehouse in England opened in Oxford in 1650, and within three decades London had hundreds. These establishments were unlike the taverns that had long dominated public sociability. Because coffee was served hot and consumed in quantity, patrons remained relatively sober, and the atmosphere favored conversation over carousing. A visitor in the 1660s described a coffeehouse as a place where a man might sit for hours with a single dish of coffee and read every newspaper in the room.
That detail explains why coffeehouses became associated with the spread of information. Many kept foreign and domestic newspapers on long tables, and some employed a reader to recite the news aloud for customers who could not read. Merchants gathered at particular houses to hear shipping reports; writers and scientists debated ideas that later appeared in print. Lloyd's of London, the insurance market, began in a coffeehouse, as did the Royal Society's earliest regular meetings.
The institutions were not universally welcomed. Critics complained that men neglected their work and their families, and in 1675 Charles II attempted to suppress the coffeehouses, arguing that they nurtured seditious talk. The order was withdrawn within weeks in the face of public protest — a small but telling sign that these rooms had already become part of how the city argued with itself.

Academic Talk 1（约 90 秒 · Psychology）
Audio script
Let's talk about something students often get wrong about studying — the value of being tested. Most people treat a test as a measurement, something that tells you how much you know but doesn't change what you know. Research on what we call the testing effect suggests the opposite: the act of retrieving information actually strengthens it.
Here's a typical study. Two groups read a short science article. One group reads it again; the other group is asked to write down everything it can remember. A week later, both groups take the same final test — and the retrieval group does substantially better, even though it spent the same amount of time and never saw the text a second time.
Why? Struggling to pull a fact out of memory seems to make it more accessible later. Rereading, by contrast, feels easy and fluent, and that feeling is misleading: fluency is not the same as durability. Students judge their learning by how familiar the material feels, so rereading wins their vote while testing wins the actual exam.
Two practical points. First, test yourself early, before you feel ready — getting items wrong at that stage helps, provided you check the correct answer. Second, space the practice out. Cramming produces quick improvement and rapid forgetting; distributed retrieval produces the opposite pattern.

Academic Talk 2（约 100 秒 · Environmental Science）
Audio script
If you walk from a city park into a downtown street on a summer afternoon, you can feel the difference in temperature — often several degrees within a few hundred meters. That's the urban heat island, and it isn't just a matter of comfort.
Three things drive it. First, materials: asphalt and concrete absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly at night, so cities cool down far less than surrounding countryside. Second, geometry: tall buildings trap that heat in street canyons and block the wind that would carry it away. Third, the city itself — vehicles, air conditioners and factories all add waste heat.
Notice what's missing from that list: vegetation. Plants cool their surroundings in two ways, by shading surfaces and by transpiration, releasing water vapor through their leaves. A single mature tree can be worth several small air conditioners in cooling power, and unlike an air conditioner it doesn't push waste heat outdoors.
This is why so many cities now treat tree cover as infrastructure rather than decoration. Los Angeles, for instance, set a target of planting ninety thousand trees, prioritizing neighborhoods with the least existing canopy — which, not coincidentally, tend to be the hottest and the least affluent. Melbourne has experimented with pale, reflective pavement as well.
I'd add one caution. Planting trees is slow, and the neighborhoods that need cooling most need it now. Green roofs and reflective surfaces work faster, though on a smaller scale, so most experts recommend combining all three rather than betting on one.
